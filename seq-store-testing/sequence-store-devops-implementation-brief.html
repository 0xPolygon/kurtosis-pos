Sequence Store — DevOps Implementation Brief (Copy)
For the DevOps team · sequence store

What we need built, and
what it has to satisfy

The sequence store is a new backend service that lets Polygon RPC nodes serve transaction receipts a couple of seconds earlier than they can today. It sits beside the chain and nothing on the chain depends on it. This is the short version, aimed at whoever writes the deployment code: components, artifacts, dependencies, health checks, deployment rules, alarms.

The 60-second version

Four things run. A three-node Redpanda cluster (the storage layer), and three small Go services that all ship as one image: an ingress that writes to it, gateways that read from it, and an auditor that watches for anomalies. One L4 load balancer fronts the gateways; Bor’s block producer talks to the ingress directly. Bor connects from outside.

Nothing here can affect the chain. If the whole service is down, Polygon produces blocks exactly as it does today; users simply wait the normal amount of time for receipts. That makes it a low-risk first deployment and means alarms here should never page the chain on-call rotation.

Target: Amoy first, then mainnet. Nothing is deployed anywhere yet.

Only the read path is load balanced. There is exactly one writer and exactly one producer, so on the publish side there is nothing to balance and no failover for a load balancer to perform — Bor's block producer connects straight to the ingress. That also keeps the certificate story simple: the client certificate is the writer's identity, and a direct connection reaches the ingress process intact by construction.

The read endpoint must not terminate TLS. Plain L4 passthrough, not an L7 / HTTPS balancer. We do not authenticate readers today, but we want to keep that option, and it disappears the moment something in the middle re-originates the connection.

Redpanda needs no load balancer either. The ingress takes a comma-separated -brokers list; give it all three addresses and it finds the right one itself. It is a Kafka client, so it discovers which broker leads each partition and follows leadership as it moves between them.

One thing for us to settle before you write code: the gateway's gRPC port is 9551 in the binary's defaults but 9550 in our own deployment notes. We will confirm the intended number — please don't infer it from the docs.

D

Health checks

All three services expose the same two HTTP endpoints on port 9600. This is what the read-path load balancer and your orchestration probes should use.

EndpointRole200 means503 means
GET /readyingressThis instance is the live writer and is serving.Starting up or recovering. Send it no traffic.
GET /readygatewayCaught up with the data and safe to read from.Still catching up, or fallen too far behind. Take it out of rotation.
GET /readyauditorRunning and following.Not running. Alarm on this, do not route on it.
GET /metricsall threePrometheus format, same port.

Do not wire a restart-on-failure probe to /ready on the ingress. A 503 here means "not currently the writer", which is a normal working state — during startup and catch-up today, and permanently for a standby once that ships. A liveness probe pointed at it would restart healthy instances, and on a catching-up one it would restart it before it ever finishes. Use /ready for routing only. We owe you a separate liveness endpoint; it does not exist yet.

For Redpanda itself, use its own health tooling and admin API on 9644 rather than anything we provide.

E

What a deployment has to satisfy

Six rules. Everything else is your call.

#RuleWhy it matters
1Never take more than one Redpanda node down at a time. Two of three must always be up.Writes need a majority. Two down stops the service until one returns.
2Run exactly one ingress instance. Not two, and not behind a load balancer. Point the publish name at it and let a restart policy handle process failure.Only one instance may write, and until the standby mechanism ships, a second instance does not idle — it competes. See the note below.
3Never restart all gateways at once. One at a time; keep at least two serving.A fresh gateway reads back through the stored data before it can serve — minutes, not seconds. Restart them together and reads are down for that whole time.
4Exactly one auditor. Never two.Two would double-count and produce duplicate reports.
5All three services always run the same image tag.They share internal logic; mixed versions can disagree about the stored data.
6Release order: brokers → gateways → auditor → ingress.Readers before the writer is the safe direction.

Why rule 2 says one instance and not two

Ingress is the one component where "just run two for redundancy" is the wrong instinct, so it is worth being precise about what enforces what. Three separate things are involved, and only two of them exist today.

MechanismWhat it decidesWhere it livesExists?
the fenceWhether two instances can both write. They cannot — the broker locks out the older one permanently, mid-sentence.brokeryes
readinessWhether this instance is currently the writer, which is what your health check reads./readyyes
the leaseWhich instance should try to become the writer. This is what makes a second instance sit idle instead of competing.—not yet

What readiness does on its own

The useful property is that readiness is derived, not configured. Nothing is flagged as "the standby" and no operator sets anything: the instance reports ready only while it actually holds the writer role and has finished catching up. It walks this loop by itself, and the health check simply observes where it is.

What is missing, and why it changes the rule. Start a second instance today and it does not wait politely — it immediately tries to become the writer, takes the role from the first, and the first takes it back when its next write fails. They trade the role back and forth, and publishing stalls each time. There is also a window where both answer 200: an instance that has just lost the role does not find out until it next tries to write, so until then its health check still says it is fine.

Nothing gets corrupted while that happens — layer 3 guarantees only one of them ever actually writes — but it is worse for availability than running a single instance, which is the counter-intuitive part. The lease is the missing piece that makes a second instance genuinely passive, and it is on our side to build. Until it lands: one instance, plus a restart policy. A crashed process recovers on its own in seconds.

What each restart costs

RestartingUser-visible effect
one redpanda nodenone — if you drain its leadership first
one gatewaynone — its clients reconnect elsewhere
the auditornone — it serves nobody
the ingressbrief pause — early receipts stop for seconds; chain unaffected

The ingress is the only one with unavoidable downtime, because exactly one instance may write at a time — so an upgrade is a handoff, and during a handoff nobody is writing. Seconds, and only early receipts are affected. Please schedule ingress restarts deliberately rather than letting automation do them unannounced.

G

What we would like to agree on

  1. Who owns the deployment code? Our assumption is DevOps, matching every other service, with us owning the application and its release tags. Confirm or push back.
  2. Where should the image live? GHCR as the build already produces, or mirrored into Artifact Registry to match your existing pattern? We will follow whatever you already do.
  3. Redpanda on VMs or Kubernetes? Our proposal is three dedicated VMs, provisioned like a chain node — it wants fast local disk and predictable CPU. The three Go services are small and stateless and would sit comfortably on the existing Kubernetes platform. Happy to be overruled; you know the estate better.
  4. Which project and environment for Amoy first?
  5. Certificates. Later phase, flagging now: the publish endpoint will eventually need per-validator client certificates, which means an issuing and rotation story. Not needed for the first internal deployment.
  6. Pull credentials for a private registry. The repo is private, so the published image is too — an unauthenticated pull is rejected. Whatever runs the deployment needs credentials, and how you prefer to supply those is your call.

Gaps on our side, for transparency

None of these block you from starting, but they change how good the operational story is, and they are ours to close:

  • No versioned release tag yet, so nothing immutable to pin a deployment to — :dev exists but moves on every merge blocks first deploy
  • No graceful shutdown on the ingress — makes restarts noisier than they need to be
  • No standby arbitration yet, so losing the ingress host needs someone to start it elsewhere. A process restart recovers on its own in about 0.8 s — measured under load, not estimated
  • No liveness endpoint separate from /ready
